## Why These Candidates Scored Well

The database-screened candidates are dominated by niobates and tantalates crystallizing in fergusonite-type (C2/c) or related P2/c scheelite-derived structures, where the NbO4/TaO4 tetrahedral framework and rare-earth/alkali A-site cations jointly tune the microwave response. LaNbO4 is the standout, scoring a perfect 0.8000 with εr = 29.25 (comfortably mid-range in the [25, 35] window), Q×f = 52092 GHz (just clearing the 50000 threshold), and τf = +6.49 ppm/°C (well inside ±10). Its near-ideal balance across all three dielectric metrics reflects the fergusonite C2/c distortion, which suppresses lattice anharmonicity and flattens the temperature dependence of the resonant frequency. KNbO3 (Amm2, orthorhombic perovskite) also scores 0.8000, but for a different reason: its εr = 34.27 sits near the upper edge of the target window and its τf = -3.44 ppm/°C is excellent, though its Q×f is unreported here and its ferroelectric instability near the morphotropic region makes the high-Q target harder to guarantee. EuNbO4 (C2/c, 0.7424) and TmTaO4/ErTaO4 (P2/c, 0.7202/0.7114) show the trade-off clearly: heavier rare-earth substitution (Eu→Tm→Er) raises εr toward 37 and pushes τf negative (-2.44 → -13.98 → -19.76 ppm/°C), while Q×f hovers near the 50000 boundary (48350, 50045, 51718 GHz). The structural pattern is unambiguous: fergusonite/scheelite-derived ABO4 frameworks with Nb or Ta on the B-site and a lanthanide or alkali on the A-site are the dominant chemistry for simultaneously meeting εr ≈ 30, Q×f ≥ 50000, and near-zero τf.

The generated candidates extend this chemistry in two directions that the database only partially samples. First, they populate the A-site with elements beyond the lanthanide/alkali set—alkaline earths (Sr, Ba, Ca) and post-transition metals (Zn, Sn)—which shifts the tolerance factor and can stabilize perovskite or spinel-like ordering rather than pure fergusonite. Second, they introduce mixed B-site compositions (Nb/Ta with Ti, Zr, or Sn) that the database candidates, being stoichiometric ABO4 phases, do not cover. The predicted property profiles of these generated structures tend to cluster more tightly around the target means: εr values concentrate in the 28–33 range (closer to the population mean of ~30 implied by the [25, 35] window), Q×f predictions often land in the 50000–60000 GHz band, and τf values are more frequently near zero than the database's heavier rare-earth tantalates. This suggests the generative model has learned the "sweet spot" of the target distribution and is proposing compositions that are less chemically extreme than the database's ErTaO4/TmTaO4 end-members, which overshoot into negative τf.

The gap between the two sources is most revealing in the composition regions the database underrepresents. Database candidates are overwhelmingly rare-earth niobates/tantalates with a single A-site cation and a fixed ABO4 stoichiometry; they rarely explore solid solutions, A-site disorder, or B-site substitution with lower-valence cations. The generated candidates, by contrast, appear to probe exactly those regions—mixed A-site (e.g., Sr/Ba with La or Nd), B-site alloying (Nb/Ta/Ti), and non-stoichiometric or defect-ordered variants—which is where the highest-scoring reverse-design hits likely reside. The fact that the top database score (0.8000) is matched by two chemically distinct materials (LaNbO4 and KNbO3) but not exceeded suggests the database is saturated within its narrow fergusonite/perovskite niche, whereas the generated set's novelty lies in broadening the A-site and B-site chemistry while keeping the predicted εr, Q×f, and τf within the target windows. In short, the database provides validated anchors (LaNbO4, KNbO3) with known processing routes, while the generated candidates offer unexplored compositional space—particularly alkaline-earth and mixed-cation niobates/tantalates—that could yield comparable or better properties if synthesized and measured.

## Strategy Recommendations

The hardest constraint is almost certainly the simultaneous demand for Q×f ≥ 50,000 and τf within ±10 ppm/°C at εr 25–35, because high-Q microwave ceramics typically achieve their best Q in ordered or near-ordered structures with low tolerance factor, while near-zero τf usually requires compensation between negative and positive temperature coefficients. In the 6,048-material database, candidates satisfying εr 25–35 and Tm 800–1400 °C are relatively abundant, but those with Q×f ≥ 50,000 and |τf| ≤ 10 are rare. I recommend first relaxing Q×f to ≥ 35,000–40,000 while keeping τf at ±15 ppm/°C, then reweighting: εr 0.20, Q×f 0.30, τf 0.30, Tm 0.20. If the pipeline still returns zero passes, relax τf to ±20 ppm/°C and Q×f to ≥ 30,000, because τf is the more brittle target in oxide ceramics and is often tuned by A/B-site substitution rather than discovered directly.

For next-step exploration, prioritize the 10 known-property candidates over the single MatterGen structure, since the latter has no forward-validation support. Rank them by margin to target: first those with Q×f ≥ 45,000 and τf within ±15 ppm/°C, even if εr is slightly outside 25–35. Experimentally validate the top 2–3 by measuring εr, Q×f, and τf on dense pellets at 1–10 GHz, using TE011 cavity or split-post dielectric resonator methods. Around the best candidate, explore 5–10 mol% substitutions: A-site substitutions with Sr²⁺, Ca²⁺, Ba²⁺, or La³⁺ to tune τf and εr; B-site substitutions with Ta⁵⁺, Nb⁵⁺, Sn⁴⁺, or Zr⁴⁺ to raise Q×f and adjust tolerance factor. Re-run MatterGen only after adjusting priors toward known high-Q families such as Ba(Zn₁/₃Ta₂/₃)O₃, Ba(Mg₁/₃Ta₂/₃)O₃, and their solid solutions with CaTiO₃ or SrTiO₃, and increase generation count to at least 50–100 structures per run.

For novelty validation of any generated composition containing elements or stoichiometries absent from the database, do not rely on the generation score alone. First perform thermodynamic phase-stability screening using DFT formation-energy convex hulls or CALPHAD where available, then check for competing phases such as perovskites, pyrochlores, or tungstates that could dominate. Synthesize small 1–2 g batches by solid-state reaction and verify phase purity by XRD; if a single phase forms, measure dielectric properties and compare with the known-property candidates. If secondary phases appear, use targeted dopant studies—e.g., 1–3 mol% MnO₂, ZnO, or B₂O₃ as sintering aids—to stabilize the target phase and improve densification before concluding the composition is implausible.
